About the Play

Antigone is one of three plays by Sophocles that tell the story of the doomed family of Oedipus of Thebes, alongside Oedipus Rex and Oedipus at Colonus. Although it was written first (around 441 BCE), the action it depicts takes place last in the mythological sequence.

The play begins in the aftermath of a failed attack on Thebes. Two brothers, Eteocles and Polynices, have killed each other in battle. King Creon, the new ruler of Thebes, decrees that Eteocles shall be buried with full honours, while Polynices (who led the assault against the city) shall be left unburied and unmourned, his corpse exposed as a warning.

Antigone, sister of both brothers, defies the royal edict and performs funeral rites for Polynices, asserting that the unwritten laws of the gods take precedence over the decrees of mortal kings. Her act of conscience sets in motion a chain of tragedy that destroys Creon's family along with her own.

The play poses questions that remain urgently alive today: When does the individual's duty to conscience override obedience to the state? Is there a law higher than the law of rulers? What is the cost of inflexibility? Whose voice counts in the city?

Sophocles was born in Colonus, near Athens, around 497 BCE and lived to the age of ninety. He is considered one of the three great tragedians of classical Athens, alongside Aeschylus and Euripides. Of the more than 120 plays he wrote, only seven survive intact.

A general, a priest, and a diplomat as well as a playwright, Sophocles was deeply embedded in the civic life of Athens at the height of its power. His innovations in dramatic technique (introducing a third actor, reducing the role of the chorus, and developing individual character psychology) that transformed Greek theatre.

He won first prize at the Dionysia festival at least 18 times and was never awarded less than second place. Antigone was reportedly so admired by the Athenians that Sophocles was elected as a general shortly after its performance.

Antigone was first performed around 441 BCE at the City Dionysia, the great annual festival in honour of Dionysus held in Athens each spring. Greek tragedies were civic events: performances were free, part of a religious and democratic ritual, attended by thousands of citizens.

The play was composed at the apogee of Athenian democracy under Pericles, at a time of intense debate about the limits of state power, the rights of the individual, and the relationship between human law and divine order. The Periclean building programme, which gave Athens the Parthenon, was underway; the Athenian empire was at its most confident.

By invoking the unwritten laws of the gods against Creon's written decree, Sophocles tapped directly into live political anxieties. The play has since been read as a meditation on civil disobedience in every era, from Hegel's philosophy of right to the resistance movements of the 20th century.

Structure of the Play

  • I Prologue Antigone reveals Creon's decree to Ismene and resolves to bury Polynices alone.
  • II Parodos The Chorus of Theban elders enters, celebrating the city's victory in battle.
  • III Episode I Creon announces his edict; a guard reports that someone has buried Polynices.
  • IV Stasimon I The Chorus sings the "Ode to Man" (Πολλὰ τὰ δεινά) on human ingenuity and its limits.
  • V Episode II Antigone is arrested and brought before Creon; she defends her act openly. Ismene tries to share her guilt.
  • VI Stasimon II The Chorus laments the curse that follows the house of Oedipus through generations.
  • VII Episode III Haemon pleads with his father to spare Antigone; Creon refuses. Haemon warns of consequences.
  • VIII Stasimon III The Chorus sings of Eros, the force that undoes even the wisest: the "Ode to Love".
  • IX Episode IV Antigone is led to her living tomb. She laments her fate; Creon ignores her plea.
  • X Stasimon IV The Chorus recalls mythological figures similarly imprisoned: Danae, Lycurgus, Cleopatra.
  • XI Episode V Teiresias arrives with dire prophecy. Creon, shaken, finally relents and moves to free Antigone.
  • XII Hyporchema The Chorus invokes Dionysus, patron deity of Thebes, in a prayer for salvation.
  • XIII Exodos A messenger reports: Antigone has hanged herself; Haemon and Eurydice have both died. Creon is left alone in ruin.

Characters

Antigone

The protagonist and daughter of Oedipus. She bravely defies her uncle's decree to bury her brother, an act of familial and divine duty that drives the tragedy.

Creon

The King of Thebes and Antigone's uncle. He rules with strict political authority and decrees that the "traitor" Polyneices will not receive a proper burial.

Ismene

Antigone's sister. She is initially timid and refuses to help break the law out of fear, but later attempts to share the blame to stand by her sister.

Polyneices

Antigone's brother. He attacks his home city of Thebes after being denied the throne and is left unburied as punishment for his treachery.

Eteocles

Antigone's brother. He defends Thebes in the civil war and is granted an honorable burial, which causes the initial conflict between Antigone and Creon.

Haemon

The son of Creon and Antigone's fiancé. He tries to reason with his father and ultimately takes his own life after Antigone's death.

Eurydice

Creon's wife and Haemon's mother. She appears at the end of the play and commits suicide after learning of her son's death, cursing Creon with her last breath.

Tiresias

The blind prophet or seer of Thebes. He warns Creon that the gods are angry about the unburied body, urging him to reverse his decree before it is too late.

The Chorus

A group of Theban elders who provide commentary, context, and background on the events unfolding in the story, serving as the moral voice of the community.

The Sentry

A guard who reluctantly captures Antigone burying her brother and brings her before King Creon, fearing punishment for having failed to prevent it.

Messenger

Characters who deliver critical news to the stage, including reports of the suicides of Antigone, Haemon, and Eurydice.

Nurse

Present in Jean Anouilh's 20th-century adaptation of the play, an affectionate maternal figure who takes care of the sisters in their daily lives.

Themes

The central conflict of the play: Antigone upholds the unwritten, eternal laws of the gods; Creon upholds the written decree of the state. Sophocles does not resolve the tension; he dramatises its tragic cost.

Antigone is perhaps the earliest literary figure to consciously choose disobedience on grounds of conscience. Her act has been invoked by every tradition of political resistance from Stoic philosophy to modern human-rights discourse.

Antigone's primary motivation is the obligation owed to the dead, especially family. For the ancient Greeks, leaving a body unburied was a denial of humanity and an act of deepest impiety. Her loyalty is to bonds of blood over bonds of state.

Creon's tragedy is not cruelty but rigidity: the man who will not hear counsel, who equates obedience with virtue. Sophocles traces how absolute certainty curdles into tyranny, and how tyranny devours the tyrant.

Every character in the play appeals to justice, yet each means something different by it. Creon invokes civic justice; Antigone invokes divine justice; the Chorus watches helplessly as the two collide. Sophocles refuses to crown a single version as correct.

The house of Oedipus carries a curse across generations. Antigone and Creon alike are partly caught in forces larger than themselves. The Chorus reminds us that human greatness and human ruin are close neighbours.

Antigone's defiance is an assertion of personal freedom against state compulsion. Her choice to act alone, knowing the penalty, raises the question of what it means to be free within a political community that demands obedience.

Antigone knowingly walks toward death. Haemon follows her. Even Eurydice, Creon's wife, chooses death over living with his guilt. The play is saturated with characters who give up life in the name of something they hold higher.

Teiresias arrives in Episode V as the voice of divine foresight. His prophecies are never wrong in Sophocles, and Creon's refusal to heed them seals his fate. The gods speak clearly; mortals choose not to listen.

The Hyporchema is a direct prayer to Dionysus, patron of Thebes, pleading for salvation. Prayer in the play is both sincere and futile: the gods have already set events in motion that human appeals cannot reverse.

Creon's fury at Antigone is partly fury at being defied by a woman. He repeatedly frames his refusal in gendered terms. Sophocles gives Antigone (and to a degree Ismene) a complex interiority that challenges the social order of fifth-century Athens.
